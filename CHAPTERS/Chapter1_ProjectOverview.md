# CHAPTER 1: PROJECT OVERVIEW

## 1.1 Introduction to Alzheimer's Disease

### 1.1.1 Definition

Alzheimer's Disease (AD) is a progressive, neurodegenerative disorder that primarily affects the cerebral cortex and hippocampus. It is the most common form of dementia, accounting for 60-80% of all dementia cases. The disease is characterized by progressive cognitive decline, memory loss, and behavioral changes that eventually lead to complete loss of cognitive functions.

### 1.1.2 Historical Context

- **1901**: Dr. Alois Alzheimer first described the disease
- **1910**: The disease was officially named "Alzheimer's Disease"
- **1984**: First genetic link discovered (APOE4 gene)
- **2003**: Amyloid Hypothesis becomes dominant theory
- **2023**: Lecanemab approved as first disease-modifying treatment

### 1.1.3 Epidemiology

**Current Statistics:**
- **World Prevalence**: 50 million people living with dementia (2020)
- **USA**: 6.5 million people with Alzheimer's Disease
- **Europe**: 10+ million people affected
- **Annual Cost**: $305 billion globally (2020)
- **Mortality Rate**: 3rd leading cause of death in USA (after heart disease and cancer)

**Age-related Incidence:**
- **65-74 years**: 3% prevalence
- **75-84 years**: 17% prevalence
- **85+ years**: 32% prevalence

### 1.1.4 Risk Factors

**Non-Modifiable Risk Factors:**
- Age (increases after 65)
- Genetics (APOE4 gene)
- Family history
- Head trauma history

**Modifiable Risk Factors:**
- Cognitive inactivity
- Physical inactivity
- Smoking
- Hypertension
- High cholesterol
- Diabetes
- Depression
- Poor sleep quality
- Social isolation

### 1.1.5 Pathophysiology

**Key Pathological Features:**

1. **Amyloid-Beta (Aβ) Accumulation**
   - Forms plaques between neurons
   - Disrupts neural communication
   - Triggers inflammatory response

2. **Tau Protein Tangles**
   - Hyperphosphorylated tau proteins
   - Form neurofibrillary tangles
   - Damage microtubules

3. **Neuroinflammation**
   - Glial cell activation
   - Release of pro-inflammatory cytokines
   - Neuronal death and atrophy

4. **Synaptic Dysfunction**
   - Loss of synaptic connections
   - Reduced neurotransmitters (acetylcholine)
   - Impaired plasticity

**Cascade Hypothesis:**
```
Aβ Accumulation → Tau Tangles → Inflammation → 
Synaptic Loss → Neuronal Death → Cognitive Decline
```

## 1.2 Understanding MCI (Mild Cognitive Impairment)

### 1.2.1 Definition

Mild Cognitive Impairment (MCI) is an intermediate stage between normal aging and dementia. Individuals with MCI experience noticeable cognitive decline beyond normal aging but maintain the ability to perform daily activities.

### 1.2.2 Diagnostic Criteria for MCI

**Core Features:**
1. Cognitive complaint from patient or informant
2. Cognitive impairment (documented on testing)
3. Preserved general cognition
4. Intact activities of daily living
5. Not meeting criteria for dementia

**Assessment Tools:**
- Mini-Cog Test
- Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA)
- Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE)
- Clock Drawing Test

### 1.2.3 Subtypes of MCI

1. **Amnestic MCI (aMCI)**
   - Primary memory impairment
   - Highest risk for AD conversion (10-15% per year)

2. **Non-Amnestic MCI (naMCI)**
   - Other cognitive domains affected
   - Executive function, language, visuospatial
   - Lower conversion rate (2-4% per year)

3. **Single Domain vs. Multiple Domain**
   - Single: One cognitive domain affected
   - Multiple: More than one domain affected
   - Multiple domain = higher risk

### 1.2.4 Progression Rates

| Stage | Conversion Rate | Time to AD |
|-------|-----------------|-----------|
| Normal Cognition | 1-2% per year | 10-15 years |
| MCI | 10-15% per year | 3-7 years |
| Mild AD | 20-30% per year | 2-4 years |
| Moderate AD | Stable | - |

## 1.3 Difference Between Normal Aging, MCI, and Alzheimer's

### 1.3.1 Cognitive Comparison

| Aspect | Normal Aging | MCI | Alzheimer's |
|--------|-------------|-----|------------|
| Memory Loss | Occasional forgetfulness | Noticeable decline | Severe memory loss |
| Activities | Performs all daily activities | Mostly independent | Requires assistance |
| Awareness | Aware of changes | Aware of problems | May deny problems |
| Progression | Stable | Gradual decline | Rapid decline |
| Assessment | Normal cognitive tests | Mild abnormalities | Significant impairment |

### 1.3.2 Clinical Progression Timeline

```
Age 65
   |
   ├─→ Normal Cognition (No disease markers)
   |       |
   |       └─→ 1-2% annual conversion
   |
   ├─→ Asymptomatic Alzheimer's (Biomarkers present)
   |       |
   |       └─→ 5-10% annual symptomatic conversion
   |
   ├─→ MCI due to AD (Cognitive + Biomarkers)
   |       |
   |       └─→ 10-15% annual conversion
   |
   └─→ Dementia due to AD (Full diagnosis)
           |
           └─→ 3-10 year survival after diagnosis
```

### 1.3.3 Neuroimaging Differences

**Normal Brain:**
- Gray matter: Well-preserved
- Ventricular size: Normal
- Hippocampus: Normal volume
- EEG: Regular oscillations

**MCI Brain:**
- Gray matter: Mild atrophy
- Ventricular size: Slightly enlarged
- Hippocampus: Mild volume reduction
- EEG: Slowed oscillations in alpha band

**Alzheimer's Brain:**
- Gray matter: Significant atrophy
- Ventricular size: Enlarged
- Hippocampus: Severe volume loss
- EEG: Severely slowed activity

## 1.4 Why EEG is Used Instead of MRI/PET

### 1.4.1 Comparison of Neuroimaging Modalities

| Aspect | EEG | MRI | PET | fMRI |
|--------|-----|-----|-----|------|
| Cost | $ | $$$$ | $$$$ | $$$$ |
| Availability | High | Medium | Low | Low |
| Portability | Yes | No | No | No |
| Radiation | No | No | Yes (tracer) | No |
| Time Resolution | 1 ms | 1 second | Minutes | Seconds |
| Spatial Resolution | Poor | Excellent | Fair | Good |
| Ease of Use | Simple | Complex | Complex | Complex |
| Real-time | Yes | No | No | No |
| Repeated Measures | Easy | Limited | Limited | Limited |

### 1.4.2 EEG Advantages

**Cost-Effectiveness:**
- Equipment cost: $5,000-$50,000
- Per-patient cost: $100-$500
- vs. MRI: $1,000-$5,000 per scan
- vs. PET: $3,000-$8,000 per scan

**Clinical Accessibility:**
- Can be used in clinics, hospitals, homes
- No special infrastructure needed
- Portable and bedside capable
- No waiting lists

**Sensitivity to Functional Changes:**
- Detects electrophysiological changes
- Reveals cognitive processing abnormalities
- Can detect changes before structural MRI
- Sensitive to neurochemical changes

**Practical Advantages:**
- Non-invasive, no radiation
- Repeatable without safety concerns
- Quick acquisition (10-30 minutes)
- No contraindications (pacemakers, implants safe)
- Real-time monitoring capability

### 1.4.3 Limitations of MRI/PET

**MRI Limitations:**
- Expensive ($1,000-$5,000 per scan)
- Slow detection of functional changes
- Not suitable for elderly with implants
- Requires special training
- Claustrophobia issues

**PET Limitations:**
- Uses radioactive tracers
- Very expensive ($5,000-$10,000)
- Radiation exposure
- Limited availability
- Not suitable for repeated measures

### 1.4.4 EEG Sensitivity to AD Markers

**EEG can detect:**
1. **Slowing of oscillations** - Shift to lower frequencies
2. **Reduced connectivity** - Weakened synchronization
3. **Spectral changes** - Increased delta, decreased alpha
4. **Complexity reduction** - Simplified patterns
5. **Cholinergic dysfunction** - Reduced acetylcholine effects

## 1.5 Research Problem

### 1.5.1 Problem Statement

Despite advances in neurology and cognitive science, early diagnosis of Alzheimer's Disease remains challenging. Current diagnostic methods rely on:

- Cognitive testing (subjective, operator-dependent)
- Structural neuroimaging (expensive, not always conclusive)
- Biomarker analysis (invasive, time-consuming)
- Clinical symptoms (appear after neurodegeneration)

**Core Problem:**
Many patients are diagnosed with dementia only after significant cognitive decline has occurred. By this stage, neurodegeneration is extensive, and therapeutic interventions have limited effectiveness. Earlier detection during MCI or preclinical stages could enable timely intervention and potentially slow disease progression.

### 1.5.2 Current Diagnostic Challenges

1. **Lack of Objective Biomarkers**
   - Diagnosis based on cognitive assessment
   - High inter-rater variability
   - Subjective interpretation

2. **Limited Accessibility**
   - Advanced imaging expensive
   - Not available in resource-poor settings
   - Long waiting times

3. **Late-Stage Detection**
   - Most diagnoses made at moderate-to-severe stage
   - Irreversible neuronal damage already occurred
   - Limited treatment options

4. **Disease Heterogeneity**
   - Mixed pathologies common
   - Atypical presentations
   - Variable progression rates

### 1.5.3 Research Gap

**Opportunity:** Develop an automated, objective, cost-effective method using machine learning and deep learning on EEG signals that:

- **Early Detection**: Identify AD/MCI before severe symptoms
- **Objectivity**: Remove subjective assessment bias
- **Accessibility**: Low-cost, portable solution
- **Accuracy**: High sensitivity and specificity
- **Reproducibility**: Consistent across users and settings

## 1.7 Scope of Project

### 1.7.1 Included Scope

**Dataset:**
- Public EEG datasets (ADNI, OASIS, PhysioNet)
- 3 classes: Normal, MCI, Alzheimer's
- Resting-state EEG recordings
- 64-electrode standard montage

**Signal Processing:**
- EEG data from 0-30 Hz (delta to low-gamma)
- ICA-based artifact removal
- Bandpass filtering
- Windowing and segmentation

**Features:**
- 40+ time-domain features
- 30+ frequency-domain features
- Wavelet-based features
- Non-linear complexity measures

**Models:**
- 6 traditional ML algorithms
- 5 deep learning architectures
- Cross-validation and LOSO
- Hyperparameter optimization

**Evaluation:**
- Classification metrics
- Statistical significance testing
- ROC curves and AUC analysis
- Confusion matrices

**Application:**
- Flask web application
- File upload and processing
- Real-time prediction
- Results visualization

### 1.7.2 Excluded Scope

**Not Included:**
- Brain imaging (MRI, PET) integration
- Genetic analysis
- Longitudinal follow-up studies
- Clinical validation with new patients
- Mobile app development
- Real-time continuous monitoring
- Multimodal data fusion
- Advanced neuroimaging processing

### 1.7.3 Limitations

**Data Limitations:**
- Limited sample size (typical datasets: 100-500 subjects)
- Class imbalance common
- Limited demographic diversity
- Missing electrode recordings in some datasets

**Technical Limitations:**
- Computational requirements for DL
- Model interpretability challenges
- Hyperparameter sensitivity
- Overfitting risk with limited data

**Clinical Limitations:**
- EEG alone may not capture all AD pathology
- No direct measurement of amyloid/tau
- Subject to individual variations
- Requires trained personnel for acquisition
